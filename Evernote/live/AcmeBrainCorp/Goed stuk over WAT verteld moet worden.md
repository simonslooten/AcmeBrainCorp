# Goed stuk over WAT verteld moet worden

Most AI risk presentations fail before the second slide.
Not because the risks are wrong.
Because the audience was never the right one.
Boards do not manage risk. They manage the business consequences of risk.
That distinction is everything.
A CISO prepared a 14-slide AI risk deck last year. Threat landscape. OWASP LLM Top 10. Governance gaps. Control recommendations.
The board sat through all of it.
Then the chair asked: "So are we comfortable proceeding with the AI rollout or not?"
The CISO had not included a slide on that question.
The rollout was approved without the controls in place.
Three months later, an AI tool with unchecked data access surfaced confidential salary information to a line manager who had no business seeing it. HR crisis. Legal review. Two senior staff resigned.
The board's follow-up question was not about the technology.
It was: "Why were we not told this was a possibility?"
They were. On slide seven. In a bullet point labelled "data leakage risk: medium."
Medium meant nothing to them. An HR crisis did.
The gap is not awareness. It is translation.
Four practical steps for translating AI risk into board decisions:
1. Start with the asset, not the threat.
What does this AI tool touch? Customer data? Financial records? Employee information? Lead with what the board already has a duty to protect.
2. Quantify the disruption window.
Not the probability of a breach. The cost of the response. "If this scenario occurs, we are looking at 3 to 6 weeks of disruption and a mandatory ICO notification. Here is what that looks like operationally."
3. Make the risk appetite decision explicit.
Present a clear either/or. Either we deploy with these four controls in place, or we accept this specific residual risk in writing. Boards can decide. They cannot act on a heat map.
4. Name the regulatory exposure.
For financial services, AI governance is no longer optional. FCA expectations are hardening. Frame the control investment against the cost of regulatory scrutiny, not against the threat itself.
Boards are not ignoring AI risk.
They are being handed information they cannot convert into decisions.
The CISO who fixes that translation problem does not slow AI adoption down.
They become the reason it happens with confidence instead of hope.
